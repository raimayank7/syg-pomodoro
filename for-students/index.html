Pomodoro for students

Sitting with a book open for three hours is not the same as studying for three hours. Short timed rounds make the difference visible.

Why it suits study

Most students measure study in hours: "I studied for four hours today". The trouble is that the four hours include messages, snacks, staring at the page and looking for a pen. A pomodoro is a smaller and more honest unit. In 25 minutes of one task with nothing else allowed, you know exactly what you did. Six real pomodoros often cover more than a whole distracted evening.

Give every round a job

Before you press Start, say what this round is for. A round without a job turns into slow reading. Good jobs look like this:

  • Read pages 41 to 48 of the history chapter and write five points from memory.
  • Solve ten sums from Exercise 3.4 and mark the ones that went wrong.
  • Learn the first eight reactions on the list and test myself with the page covered.
  • Write one answer to a past paper question in exam conditions.

Notice that each job ends with something you produce: points, solved sums, a written answer. Reading alone feels like study but leaves little behind. A simple pattern for one round is: about 15 minutes of reading or solving, then the last 10 minutes with the book closed, writing or saying what you remember.

A plan for a school day

After school, most students have between two and four hours. A workable evening looks like this:

  1. One pomodoro for the homework that must be handed in tomorrow. Start with it so that it does not sit on your mind.
  2. Two pomodoros for the hardest subject while you are still fresh.
  3. A long break for food and rest.
  4. Two pomodoros for a second subject.
  5. One short round to look again at what you learned yesterday. Revising after a day helps it stay.

That is six pomodoros: two and a half hours of real study in about three and a half hours on the clock. The day planner turns a list like this into a timetable with the break times worked out.

Mix subjects the right way

Do not change subject every round; you lose time warming up. Do not stay on one subject for eight rounds either; attention fades. Two or three rounds on a subject, then a change, suits most people. Put a writing or problem-solving subject after a reading subject, so that the kind of work changes too.

The phone

This is the hardest part and the most important. During a round, the phone is silent, face down and out of reach: in another room if possible. If you use this site's timer on the phone itself, turn on Do Not Disturb first. You will check messages in the break only if you choose to, and many students find it better to keep the phone for the long break. The page on phone distraction has a full routine.

Count, do not judge

At night, count the day's pomodoros and write the number down. Do not compare it with a friend's number or with a topper's interview. Compare it with your own last week. Going from four a day to six a day over a month is real progress, and it is the kind you can keep.

Questions people ask

How many pomodoros should a student do in a day?

There is no fixed number. On a school day, four to eight is realistic for most students. On a holiday, ten to fourteen is a full day. Start with a number you can repeat every day and raise it slowly.

Is 25 minutes too short for maths or physics?

For long problems it can be. Try two rounds back to back on the same problem set, or use a 50 minute round with a 10 minute break. Keep the rule the same: one task, no switching.

Can I listen to music while studying?

If it has no words and you stop noticing it, it does little harm for practice work. For reading and memorising, most people do better in quiet. Test both for a week and count your rounds.
