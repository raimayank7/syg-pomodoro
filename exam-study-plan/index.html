How to make an exam study plan in pomodoros

A syllabus feels endless until you count it. Counted in pomodoros, it becomes a number you can divide by the days you have.

Step 1: List every chapter

Take the official syllabus of your board, university or examination and write every chapter or unit of every subject in one list. Use the syllabus itself, not a guide book's contents page, so that nothing is added or missed. If the list fills four pages, good: now you can see the whole job.

Step 2: Estimate each chapter in pomodoros

Against each chapter write three numbers: pomodoros to read and understand it, pomodoros to practise questions, and pomodoros to revise it later. A light chapter may be 2 + 2 + 1. A heavy one may be 6 + 8 + 3. Your first estimates will be wrong, and that is fine. After a week of real study you will correct them. The page on estimating tasks explains how to get better at this.

Step 3: Count the days honestly

Count the days from today to the exam. Then take away the days that will not be study days: school events, travel, festivals, the day before each paper. Decide how many pomodoros you can truly do on a school day and on a holiday. Multiply and add. This is your supply.

A worked example: 60 days remain. Ten will be lost. Of the 50 left, 35 are school days at 6 pomodoros and 15 are holidays at 12. Supply = 35 × 6 + 15 × 12 = 210 + 180 = 390 pomodoros.

Step 4: Compare supply with demand

Add up the estimates from Step 2. Suppose they come to 340. You have 390, so there is a margin of 50 pomodoros. Keep it as a buffer; do not fill it. If demand is larger than supply, you now know two months early instead of two days early. You can then choose: add one round a day, trim the practice of chapters you already know, or decide which low-weight topics get a single reading only.

Step 5: Lay the chapters on the calendar

  • Put the hardest and highest-weight chapters first, while there is time to repeat them.
  • Study two or three subjects a day, not one. The planner can take turns between subjects for you.
  • Give each week one lighter day. Use it to finish what slipped.
  • Stop new chapters some days before the exam and keep the final stretch for revision and past papers only.

Step 6: Build revision into the plan

A chapter read once is mostly forgotten in a few weeks. Plan to meet each chapter again: a short round the next day, another after about a week, another after about a month. These rounds are short because you are recalling, not learning afresh. Close the book, write what you remember, then open it and check what you missed.

Step 7: Past papers under the clock

In the last weeks, sit full past papers in the real time limit. A three hour paper is not done in pomodoros; it is done in one sitting, as in the hall. Afterwards, use pomodoros to go through every mistake and write the correct answer.

Keep the plan alive

Every Sunday, spend one pomodoro on the plan itself. Tick what is done, move what slipped, and correct your estimates. A plan that is checked weekly survives. A perfect plan made once and never opened again is a decoration. The page on weekly planning gives a routine for this review.

Check the official syllabus. Syllabus, paper pattern and dates come only from your board, university or examining body. Download the current year's document from the official website before you plan.

Questions people ask

How many pomodoros does a chapter take?

It depends on the chapter and on you. Time your first few chapters and write down the real count. After that, estimate new chapters by comparing them with ones you have finished.

What if I fall behind the plan?

Use the weekly lighter day and the buffer first. If you are still behind, reduce the plan openly: fewer practice rounds for strong chapters. Do not try to catch up by cancelling sleep; tired rounds are worth little.

Should I plan every hour of every day?

No. Plan the number of pomodoros per subject per day, and choose the exact hours each morning. A plan that is too tight breaks on the first disturbed day.
