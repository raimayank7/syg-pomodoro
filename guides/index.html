Pomodoro

Guides to studying and working in timed rounds

19 guides in plain words. Each one covers a single question in full and ends with the next step.

  • How the Pomodoro method works One task, one timer, one short break. The method is simple on purpose, and the simple rules are what make it work.
  • Pomodoro for students Sitting with a book open for three hours is not the same as studying for three hours. Short timed rounds make the difference visible.
  • How to make an exam study plan in pomodoros A syllabus feels endless until you count it. Counted in pomodoros, it becomes a number you can divide by the days you have.
  • Pomodoro at work An office day is cut into pieces by meetings, messages and people. The method does not remove them. It helps you protect a few solid rounds between them.
  • Pomodoro when you work from home At home nobody tells you when work starts, when it pauses and when it ends. The timer can do that job.
  • How to handle interruptions during a pomodoro You cannot stop interruptions from arriving. You can decide what happens in the ten seconds after one arrives.
  • What to do in a pomodoro break The break decides the quality of the next round. Five good minutes are worth more than fifteen spent scrolling.
  • How to stop the phone from breaking your focus Willpower loses to a phone that is within reach. Change where the phone is, and you need far less willpower.
  • How to stop putting work off and start You do not need to feel ready. You need to work on the thing for 25 minutes. Feeling ready usually arrives about ten minutes in.
  • How to estimate tasks in pomodoros Most plans fail because the tasks take longer than we hoped. Counting rounds teaches you how long your own work really takes.
  • How to split big tasks and group small ones A task is the right size when you can see its end from where you start. Most tasks on a to-do list are either too big or too small for that.
  • Pomodoro, deep work and flow Some work needs an hour just to load into your head. The method bends to fit that; the principles stay the same.
  • Pomodoro for coding and programmers Programming punishes interruption more than most work. A little structure around each round protects the thread and stops rabbit holes.
  • Pomodoro for writing Writing stalls when you try to think, draft and correct at the same moment. Give each of those its own round.
  • Pomodoro for long study days and competitive exams Preparation for a competitive examination is a matter of months. The plan that wins is the one you can repeat on a dull Tuesday.
  • When the Pomodoro method does not work: problems and fixes When the method fails, the cause is nearly always one of a dozen ordinary things, and each has an ordinary fix.
  • Group study with a shared pomodoro timer Studying next to someone who is working makes it easier to keep working. A shared timer turns a chatty group into a useful one.
  • A homework timer for children Children can concentrate well for a short time. Ask for that short time, make it visible, and let them move in between.
  • How to plan your week in pomodoros A day is too short to see where your time goes and a month is too long to correct it. The week is the right size.

Where to begin

If the method is new to you, read how it works first. It takes five minutes and gives you the six steps and the two rules that everything else depends on. Then run a set of four rounds with the timer before reading further. The guides make much more sense once you have felt a bell ring in the middle of a task.

After that, choose by your situation. Students should read the student guide and, before examinations, the exam study plan. People with office jobs should start with pomodoro at work. Parents will find round lengths and break ideas in a homework timer for children. If you have tried the method and given up, go straight to common problems and fixes.

How the guides fit together

Three guides cover the daily mechanics: interruptions, breaks and phone distraction. Three cover planning: splitting tasks, estimating and weekly planning. The rest apply the method to one kind of work or one kind of person. You do not need to read them all. Pick the one that matches today's problem, change one thing, and come back when the next problem appears.
